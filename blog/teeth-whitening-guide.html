Teeth Whitening: What Actually Works, and What Just Wears Enamel

US Dr. Usha Sribollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics July 10, 2026 5 min read

Almost everything sold as whitening does one of two completely different things. It either removes stain sitting on the surface of your teeth, or it changes the colour of the tooth structure itself. Understanding which is which explains why some products work brilliantly, some do nothing, and a few do quiet damage.

The short version

  • Only peroxide changes the actual colour of a tooth. Everything else removes surface stain, which is useful but limited.
  • Abrasive whitening products, including charcoal, can polish away stain while thinning enamel, and thinner enamel eventually looks more yellow, not less.
  • Custom trays from a dentist are the best balance of result, cost, and control for most people.
  • Whitening does not change the colour of crowns, veneers, or fillings, which matters a great deal if you have any on your front teeth.
  • Grey discolouration from within a tooth, and tetracycline staining, respond poorly to bleaching and need a different approach.

Two completely different mechanisms

Stain sits in two places, and this is the whole basis of the confusion.

Extrinsic stain is on the outside of the enamel. Coffee, tea, red wine, curry, tobacco, and some mouthwashes deposit pigment onto the surface, where it accumulates in the microscopic texture of the enamel. It can be physically removed, which is what a hygiene appointment does and what whitening toothpaste attempts.

Intrinsic colour is the colour of the tooth itself, mainly determined by the dentin underneath the enamel. It darkens naturally with age, as enamel thins and dentin thickens. It cannot be scrubbed off, because it is not on the surface. Changing it requires a chemical that penetrates the enamel, and in practice that means hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide.

The single most useful sentence on this page

If a product does not contain peroxide, it cannot make your teeth whiter than their natural colour. It can only return them to their natural colour by removing stain.

Working through the options

Whitening toothpaste

Mostly abrasives, sometimes with enzymes or low-level peroxide. They remove and slow surface stain, which is a legitimate benefit, and for someone whose teeth are naturally light and simply coffee-stained, it may be all they need. It will not change your underlying shade. Look for a reasonable relative dentin abrasivity value and treat anything advertising dramatic results with scepticism.

Charcoal powders and pastes

These are abrasive, and that is the entire mechanism. They polish stain off, which produces a visible short-term improvement, and people understandably conclude it is working. The problem is that enamel does not regenerate, and repeated abrasion thins it. Thinner enamel is more translucent, which lets the yellower dentin show through, so long-term use tends to make teeth look darker. Most also lack fluoride. I would not recommend them.

Baking soda, lemon juice, and other kitchen remedies

Baking soda is mildly abrasive and reasonably harmless occasionally. Lemon juice, vinegar, and anything acidic actively dissolves enamel, and combining acid with abrasion is the worst possible pairing. Please do not.

Over-the-counter whitening strips

These do contain peroxide, so they genuinely work. They are the best value option available without a dentist. The limitations are that concentrations are low so results are modest and slow, the strips do not adapt to your tooth shape so coverage is uneven, and they often miss the gumline and the back edges of the front teeth. Reasonable for mild, even discolouration.

LED home kits

Highly variable. Some contain a decent peroxide gel and work about as well as strips. Others contain almost no active ingredient and rely entirely on the light, which does very little on its own. The one-size mouthpieces also allow gel to sit on the gums, which is the main cause of the burning and white patches people report. Read the ingredient list, not the marketing.

Custom trays from a dentist

This is what I recommend for most people, and it is what I would choose myself. Trays are made from an impression or scan of your own teeth, so the gel sits precisely on the enamel and off the gums. You get a properly formulated gel at a suitable strength, worn for a set time over one to two weeks at home, with the ability to slow down if sensitivity appears. It reaches the whole tooth evenly, including the gumline. And once you own the trays, top-ups cost the price of a syringe of gel.

In-office whitening

A high-concentration gel applied with the gums carefully isolated, usually in a single appointment of an hour or two. The advantage is speed, which matters if you have an event in ten days. Sensitivity afterwards is more common and more noticeable. In practice, the most durable results usually come from an in-office session followed by custom trays for maintenance.

What whitening will not fix

This is the part that gets skipped in advertising, and it is where disappointment comes from.

Peroxide has no effect whatsoever on crowns, veneers, bridges, or composite fillings. If you have a filling on a front tooth, whitening the surrounding enamel will make that filling stand out, and it will need replacing to match afterwards. This should always be planned before you start, not discovered after.

A single tooth that has gone grey or dark after trauma is discoloured from the inside, often because the nerve has died. Surface whitening barely touches it. That situation needs assessment of the nerve and usually internal whitening or a crown.

Tetracycline staining, which produces banded grey or brown discolouration from antibiotics taken in childhood, responds poorly and slowly to bleaching. Veneers are often the realistic answer.

Enamel that is already thin from erosion will look greyer and more translucent rather than whiter, because you are seeing through it. And white spots from fluorosis or early demineralisation sometimes become more obvious before they blend in.

Get examined before you whiten

Peroxide reaching an untreated cavity, an exposed root surface, or a cracked tooth causes significant pain, and whitening over active gum inflammation is uncomfortable and ineffective. A check-up first is not an upsell, it is the reason home kits go wrong.

Making the result last

The habits are unremarkable but they work. Drink coffee, tea, and red wine with a glass of water alongside, and finish with the water. A straw genuinely helps for iced coffee and cola. Do not brush immediately after anything acidic, wait half an hour. Keep up regular hygiene appointments, since professional cleaning removes the stain that whitening cannot. And if you have trays, one top-up night every six to twelve months keeps you where you finished.

My honest recommendation

If your teeth are basically the shade you want and just stained, book a hygiene appointment and use a decent low-abrasion toothpaste. That is cheaper and more effective than any whitening product.

If you want your teeth genuinely lighter, get custom trays. The cost sits between strips and in-office whitening, the result is better and more even than strips, and you keep the trays for life.

And if your concern is a dark single tooth, an old filling that no longer matches, or staining that has not responded to anything, whitening is probably not the right tool. That is a restorative question, and it is worth having someone look properly rather than spending another few months on products that were never going to work.
