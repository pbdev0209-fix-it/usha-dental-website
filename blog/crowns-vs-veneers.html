Crowns vs Veneers: Which One Does Your Tooth Actually Need?

US Dr. Usha Sribollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics June 5, 2026 5 min read

These two get discussed as though they were two shades of the same product. They are not. One rebuilds a tooth that has lost structural strength, the other resurfaces the front of a tooth that is already sound. Confusing them is how healthy teeth get over-treated and weak teeth get under-treated.

The short version

  • A crown covers the whole tooth and rebuilds strength. A veneer covers only the front surface and changes appearance.
  • Crowns require significantly more tooth removal. Veneers are far more conservative, and some need almost none.
  • The deciding question is how much healthy tooth is left, not how you want it to look.
  • A tooth that has had root canal treatment, a large filling, or a crack almost always needs a crown, not a veneer.
  • Veneers on teeth that grind or clench without a night guard fail early. Protecting them is part of the treatment.

The plain difference

A crown is a cap that covers the entire tooth, all the way around, down to the gumline. To make room for it, the tooth is reduced on every surface by roughly one to two millimetres. Its purpose is structural: it holds a weakened tooth together and takes the biting load that the tooth can no longer take alone.

A veneer is a thin shell bonded to the front surface only, usually under a millimetre thick, with a small wrap over the biting edge on front teeth. Its purpose is appearance: shape, colour, alignment, and proportion. It adds essentially nothing to the strength of the tooth, because it does not encircle it.

The mental model I use with patients

A crown is scaffolding around a weakened structure. A veneer is a new facade on a sound one. If the structure behind is compromised, no amount of facade fixes it.

When a tooth needs a crown

The common thread is lost structure. After root canal treatment, a back tooth is hollowed out and brittle and will eventually split without full coverage. A tooth with a very large old filling has more filling than tooth, and the remaining walls flex under load until one fractures. A cracked tooth needs to be held together, and only something that encircles it can do that. A broken cusp, a heavily worn molar, and an implant that needs a biting surface all need a crown.

In all of these, appearance is a secondary consideration. The tooth is being rebuilt because it will not survive as it is.

When a veneer is the right answer

The common thread here is that the tooth is fundamentally healthy and the concern is how it looks. Teeth that are intrinsically discoloured and have not responded to whitening. Small chips on front tooth edges. Gaps that you would rather close without orthodontics. Teeth slightly out of line where full alignment is not wanted. Worn or uneven edges. Teeth that are unusually shaped or narrow.

Veneers are a conservative option in the right situation, and one of the genuine advances of the last few decades is how little tooth some designs now need. That is exactly why applying them to teeth that are structurally compromised is a mistake: it wastes the advantage.

The question that decides it

How much healthy tooth is left.

That is the whole assessment. Not what you would like it to look like, not what you have read, and not what would be cheaper. If there is enough sound tooth to support a thin shell, a veneer is the more conservative and usually the better choice. If there is not, a veneer bonded to a weak tooth will debond or the tooth will fracture beneath it, and you will have spent money to end up needing a crown anyway, with less tooth than you started with.

Nobody can answer that question by looking in a mirror or by reading an article. It needs an examination and usually an X-ray, and it is the one part of this that genuinely requires a dentist.

What the process involves

Both usually take two appointments, and both are done under local anaesthetic if needed. At the first, the tooth is prepared, a digital scan or impression is taken, the shade is matched, and a temporary is fitted. In the laboratory, the restoration is made to fit your tooth and match the teeth around it. At the second, the temporary comes off, the fit and appearance are checked before anything is fixed permanently, adjustments are made, and then it is cemented or bonded.

You get to see and approve the appearance before it is permanently attached, particularly on front teeth. If the shape or shade is not right, that is the moment to say so, and I would much rather hear it then.

The failure I see most often

Veneers placed on someone who clenches or grinds at night, with no night guard afterwards. Porcelain is strong in compression and vulnerable to the shearing forces of grinding. If you have wear on your back teeth or you wake with a tight jaw, that needs addressing as part of the plan, not as an afterthought when something chips.

Sequence matters more than people realise

Two practical points that avoid expensive disappointment.

Whiten first, if you are going to whiten. Porcelain and composite do not respond to peroxide, so the restoration is made to match whatever shade your natural teeth are on the day. Whitening afterwards leaves the new work looking darker than everything around it and there is no way to fix that except replacing it.

Sort out alignment before resurfacing. If teeth are significantly out of position, moving them first usually means less tooth needs removing and a better final result. Building bulk onto a badly positioned tooth to make it look straight is sometimes necessary but it is rarely the best available outcome.

Why I care about this distinction

My specialist training is an MDS in prosthodontics, which is the field concerned with rebuilding and replacing teeth. The habit that training instils is to remove as little healthy tooth structure as the situation allows, because it is the one thing that cannot be replaced. Every millimetre you keep is a millimetre available for whatever the tooth needs in twenty years.

That is why I will push back on crowning a tooth that does not need it, and equally why I will not veneer a tooth that needs holding together. Both errors cost you tooth. If you have been offered one of these and you are not sure which category your tooth falls into, that is a very reasonable thing to ask, and a good dentist will show you on the X-ray rather than just telling you.

Questions patients ask me about this

With good care, both commonly last a decade or considerably more, and many last far longer. What shortens their life is grinding without protection, decay at the margin where the restoration meets the tooth, and gum recession exposing the edge. Longevity depends more on those factors than on the choice between the two.

Some very conservative designs remove almost nothing and are close to reversible, but you should ask specifically rather than assume. Anyone describing veneers as simply reversible is oversimplifying.

Sometimes, and that is a good instinct, because less tooth removal is generally better. But if the tooth has lost enough structure that it needs holding together, a veneer will not do that and it will fail, taking more tooth with it. Where there is a genuine choice I will always lean conservative. Where there is not, choosing the weaker option is a false economy.

Crowns are usually treated as a restorative procedure and are commonly covered in part, since they are restoring function. Veneers placed purely for appearance are typically classed as cosmetic and are usually not covered. Where a veneer is genuinely restoring a fractured tooth, coverage varies. The front desk can check your specific plan before you commit.

Porcelain is very stain resistant and does not respond to whitening either, which is the flip side. Composite is more prone to picking up stain over time. Whatever the material, the natural teeth around it will continue to change colour, which is why matching and timing matter and why whitening should always be done before, not after.
