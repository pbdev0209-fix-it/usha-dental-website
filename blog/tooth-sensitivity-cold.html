Why Cold Water Suddenly Hurts Your Teeth

US Dr. Usha Sribollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics August 22, 2026 5 min read

You have been drinking cold water your whole life. Then one week it makes you wince, and now you are sipping carefully on one side of your mouth like the tooth might break. Sudden cold sensitivity almost always has a specific, findable cause, and most of them are fixable.

The short version

  • Cold pain happens when the softer layer under your enamel, called dentin, loses its covering and its microscopic tubes become exposed to temperature.
  • The four usual culprits are receding gums, acid erosion, brushing too hard, and night-time clenching or grinding.
  • Sharp pain that stops within a couple of seconds is usually sensitivity. Pain that lingers, throbs, or wakes you up is a different problem and needs a look.
  • A potassium nitrate sensitivity toothpaste, used consistently for two to four weeks, helps most people noticeably.
  • Sensitivity that arrives suddenly in one specific tooth is worth an appointment rather than a wait-and-see.

What sensitivity actually is

Your tooth has three layers. Enamel on the outside, which has no nerve supply at all. Dentin underneath it, which is softer and is threaded with thousands of microscopic tubes. And the pulp in the middle, which holds the nerve and blood supply.

Those tubes in the dentin are filled with fluid, and they run directly toward the nerve. While enamel and gum tissue cover them, nothing much happens. The moment a patch of dentin is exposed, cold liquid causes that fluid to shift, the nerve reads the movement as a warning, and you get the familiar electric jolt.

So sensitivity is not really a disease. It is a coverage problem. The useful question is never why are my teeth sensitive but what stopped covering my dentin, and when.

The four things that usually cause it

1. Gums that have crept back

Enamel only covers the crown of the tooth. Below the gumline the root has a much thinner covering, and when gums recede even a millimetre or two, that surface is suddenly in contact with everything you drink. This is the single most common cause I see in adults, and it tends to affect the canines and premolars first because they sit forward in the arch.

Recession has several drivers: gum inflammation, a heavy bite on one tooth, orthodontic movement, or simply years of firm brushing. Recession itself is usually painless, which is why the sensitivity is the first thing people notice.

2. Acid wearing the enamel thin

Enamel dissolves in acid. Not dramatically, but steadily. Sparkling water, citrus, wine, kombucha, sports drinks, vinegar dressings, and reflux all lower the pH in your mouth. The pattern that does real damage is not the amount but the frequency: a can of something acidic sipped over two hours is far worse than the same can drunk in ten minutes.

Erosion tends to show up as sensitivity across several teeth at once, often with front teeth looking slightly translucent at the edges.

3. Brushing hard, or brushing right after acid

A firm brush and a scrubbing motion abrades the enamel at the gumline and pushes the gum tissue back at the same time, which is an unfortunate combination. Softened enamel is also more vulnerable, so brushing immediately after something acidic does more harm than brushing before it. If you have just had orange juice, rinse with plain water and wait half an hour.

A quick test

Look at the bristles of your toothbrush. If they splay outwards within a month of opening it, you are brushing harder than your teeth need. Electric brushes with a pressure sensor solve this almost immediately for most people.

4. Clenching and grinding at night

Sustained pressure flexes the tooth near the gumline and causes tiny structural cracks in the enamel there, which lets cold through. Grinding also wears the biting surfaces flat and exposes dentin on the tops of the molars. Most people who do it have no idea until someone points out the wear pattern.

When it is not just sensitivity

The distinction I care about most in the chair is how the pain behaves after the cold is gone.

What you noticeWhat it usually means
Sharp, immediate, gone in one or two secondsExposed dentin. Sensitivity in the ordinary sense.
Lingers for ten seconds or longer after the coldThe nerve is inflamed. Needs investigating.
Throbs on its own, or wakes you at nightIrreversible inflammation or infection. See someone promptly.
Hurts when you bite down, not when you drinkOften a crack, a high filling, or a bite issue.
One tooth, suddenly, much worse than the restDecay under a filling, a crack, or a failing restoration.

Do not wait on these

Facial swelling, pain that stops you sleeping, a bad taste with pressure around one tooth, or a tooth that suddenly stopped hurting after days of severe pain. That last one is not recovery, it usually means the nerve has died, and the problem is still there.

What genuinely helps at home

  1. Switch to a sensitivity toothpaste and stay on it. Look for potassium nitrate or stannous fluoride on the ingredients list. Give it four weeks, not four days.
  2. Stop rinsing after brushing. Spit and leave it. Rinsing washes away the fluoride you just applied. This one change helps more people than any product I can sell them.
  3. Use a soft brush and let it do the work. Small circles, light pressure, angled slightly into the gumline. Two minutes.
  4. Group your acidic drinks with meals rather than sipping them through the afternoon, and finish with water.
  5. Skip whitening for now. Get the sensitivity settled first, then whiten. Doing both at once tells you nothing about what is working.

Give that combination three to four weeks. A good proportion of people improve enough that nothing further is needed.

What can be done in the chair

If home care is not enough, there is a lot available and none of it is dramatic. A fluoride varnish or desensitising agent painted onto the exposed area seals the tubes and takes a couple of minutes. Where the notch at the gumline is deep, a small bonded filling covers it properly and lasts years. If the cause is a heavy bite, adjusting a fraction of a millimetre off the interfering surface can settle a tooth that has been complaining for months. If it is night-time grinding, a custom guard protects what is left. And where recession is significant, a gum graft is worth discussing.

The point of an appointment is not to hand you a stronger toothpaste. It is to work out which of those four causes is yours, because the fix is different for each one, and guessing wastes months.

The short answer

Sudden cold sensitivity means something recently stopped covering a patch of dentin. That is worth identifying rather than adapting around. People are remarkably good at quietly rearranging how they eat and drink to avoid one tooth, and by the time they mention it, the underlying cause has usually progressed. If cold water has started making you flinch, treat it as information rather than an inconvenience.

Enamel does not grow back, but that is not the whole picture. Most sensitivity comes from small areas of exposure that can be sealed, bonded, or covered, and the gum and bite factors behind it can be changed. Losing some enamel does not mean losing the tooth.
