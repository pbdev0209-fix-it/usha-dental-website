Your Child's First Dental Visit: The Right Age Is Earlier Than You Think

US Dr. Usha Sribollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics June 18, 2026 5 min read

Baby teeth look fine, nothing hurts, and there is always something more pressing that week. So the first dental visit slides. The recommendation from both the American Dental Association and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry is earlier than most parents expect, and the reason has very little to do with treatment.

The short version

  • The recommendation is a first visit by the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing, whichever comes first.
  • That first appointment is mostly about coaching parents and building familiarity, not about finding problems.
  • Baby teeth matter. They guide the adult teeth into position, and decay in them can affect the permanent tooth developing underneath.
  • Start brushing as soon as the first tooth appears, with a smear of fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush.
  • Sleeping with a bottle of milk or juice is the single most damaging habit for toddler teeth.

Why the first birthday

It sounds early for a child with perhaps four teeth. The logic becomes clear once you see what the appointment is actually for.

Habits are being set right now. Bottle use, night feeding, snacking patterns, and brushing routines are all forming in the first two years, and they are far easier to shape than to undo. Most of what I do at that appointment is talk to the parent.

Early decay moves fast. Baby tooth enamel is thinner than adult enamel, so decay progresses through it more quickly. A problem that would take a couple of years in an adult tooth can reach the nerve in a matter of months in a toddler.

Familiarity is easier to build than to repair. A child who has been to the dentist four times before anything is ever needed treats the chair as an ordinary place. A child whose first visit happens at four because a tooth hurts learns something quite different, and that association can last for decades.

Development can be checked. Tongue-tie, an unusual bite, delayed or unusual eruption, and habits like thumb sucking are all easier to address when noticed early.

The rule of thumb

First visit by the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing, whichever comes first. Then roughly every six months after that.

Baby teeth are not disposable

The reasoning I hear most often is that they will fall out anyway. It is an understandable assumption and it is not right, for a few concrete reasons.

Baby teeth hold the space for the adult teeth and guide them into position. Losing one early lets the neighbouring teeth drift into the gap, and the adult tooth then has nowhere to come through, which is a common route into orthodontic treatment that would not otherwise have been needed.

The adult teeth are developing directly beneath them. An infection in a baby tooth sits immediately next to the forming permanent tooth and can affect its enamel or its eruption path.

They are also being used. Children eat, speak, and learn to pronounce with these teeth for the better part of a decade, and the back baby teeth are not replaced until around age eleven or twelve. That is a long time to manage with a painful or missing tooth.

What actually happens at the appointment

For a toddler it is short, gentle, and mostly conversational. Your child usually sits on your lap, either facing you or lying back with their head towards me, which is far less intimidating than a chair. I count the teeth, check the gums and the tongue, look at how the jaws meet, and clean and apply fluoride varnish if appropriate and if they will tolerate it.

Then we talk, and this is the substantial part: how to brush a resisting toddler, how much toothpaste, what to do about bottles and night feeds, which snacks and drinks matter most, what teething is doing, and what to expect over the next six months.

The whole thing often takes fifteen or twenty minutes. There is no treatment unless something needs it, and nothing is done that the child is fighting.

Preparing them, and yourself

  1. Book a morning appointment. Tired children in the late afternoon are not at their most cooperative.
  2. Keep your language completely neutral. Avoid hurt, needle, drill, pull, and scared, including in reassurance. Saying it will not hurt introduces the idea that it might.
  3. Play at it beforehand. Count each other's teeth with a toothbrush. Let them practise on a toy. Familiarity with the idea does most of the work.
  4. Do not promise a reward for bravery. It signals that something requiring bravery is about to happen. A quiet treat afterwards is fine.
  5. Manage your own tension. Children read a parent's anxiety with remarkable accuracy. If you dread the dentist yourself, it is worth saying so to us privately so we can keep it out of the room.
  6. Bring the comfort object. Blanket, toy, whatever it is. It helps.

Home care by age

AgeWhat to do
Before teethWipe the gums with a clean damp cloth once a day
First tooth to 3 yearsSoft brush twice daily with a rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste. Parent brushes.
3 to 6 yearsPea-sized amount. Child can try, parent finishes. Teach spitting rather than rinsing.
6 years and upSupervise and check. Start flossing where teeth touch. Most children need help until around eight.

The best position for brushing a small child is standing behind them with their head tilted back against you, so you can see what you are doing. Two minutes, twice a day, and it is genuinely fine to make it a game or set it to a song.

The habit that causes the most damage

A bottle of milk, formula, or juice at bedtime or in the cot. The liquid pools around the upper front teeth for hours while saliva flow is at its lowest, and the resulting decay pattern is severe and distinctive. If a bottle is needed to settle, use plain water. Move to a cup by around twelve months, and keep juice to mealtimes in a cup rather than sipped through the day.

Snacks and drinks: frequency over quantity

This surprises parents more than anything else I say. It is not the amount of sugar that determines decay risk so much as how often it arrives. Each exposure drops the pH in the mouth and takes twenty to thirty minutes to recover. A biscuit eaten in one sitting is one acid attack. The same biscuit nibbled over an hour is a continuous one.

So the practical advice is to keep sweet things to mealtimes, offer water between meals rather than juice or milk, and be wary of the things that are sticky or slow to eat: dried fruit and fruit gums cling to teeth far longer than chocolate does, despite feeling like the healthier choice.

Sealants and fluoride varnish

Two straightforward, painless preventive measures worth knowing about. Fluoride varnish is painted onto the teeth in a couple of minutes and strengthens enamel. Sealants are a thin protective coating flowed into the deep grooves of the back molars, which is where the majority of childhood decay begins, and they are usually placed shortly after those molars come through around age six. Neither involves any drilling or discomfort, and both prevent problems rather than treating them.

If you are already behind

Plenty of the children I see are three, five, or eight at their first appointment, and that is not a reason for guilt or for delaying further. Start now. The visit will be short, we will find out where things stand, and if something needs doing we will plan it in a way that keeps your child comfortable with the process.

What matters much more than the date of the first appointment is that there is a second, a third, and a fourth. Children who grow up going regularly, before anything hurts, are the adults who come in without dreading it. That is the actual goal.

Questions patients ask me about this

That is entirely normal, and it is not a failed visit. We do as much as they will allow, keep it short and positive, and build on it next time. Sometimes the whole first appointment is sitting on your lap and having a look with a mirror. Forcing it teaches a child that the dentist is somewhere unpleasant, which costs far more in the long run than a delayed examination.

Many families are well served by a general dentist who sees children routinely, and there is real benefit in the whole family attending the same practice.

They are taken when there is a specific reason, such as teeth touching tightly enough that decay between them cannot be seen, a symptom, or a question about developing teeth.
