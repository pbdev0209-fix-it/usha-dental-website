Is It a Cavity or Just Sensitivity? How to Tell

US Dr. Usha Sri Bollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics July 28, 2026 5 min read

A tooth is bothering you and you are trying to work out whether it justifies an appointment. Sensitivity is irritating but stable. A cavity gets worse and eventually gets expensive. The difference is often clearer than people assume, and it comes down to how the pain behaves rather than how much it hurts.

The short version

  • Sensitivity is sharp, immediate, and gone within a couple of seconds. Cavity pain tends to linger, and it responds to sweet things as well as cold.
  • Sensitivity usually affects several teeth in a pattern. A cavity is usually one tooth you can point to.
  • Pain lasting more than about ten seconds after the trigger has gone suggests the nerve is inflamed, which is a different and more urgent situation.
  • Early cavities cause no symptoms at all, which is the whole reason routine check-ups and X-rays exist.
  • You cannot see most cavities yourself. Decay between teeth is invisible without an X-ray until it is already sizeable.

Start with duration, not intensity

Patients naturally describe pain by how bad it is. In the chair, what tells me more is how long it lasts after the trigger is removed. That single feature separates most of the possibilities.

Sensitivity: what it looks like

Ordinary sensitivity comes from dentin losing its covering, usually because gums have receded slightly, enamel has thinned from acid, or firm brushing has worn a notch at the gumline. There is no hole and no bacterial invasion. The nerve is healthy, just more exposed to temperature than it should be.

The pattern is characteristic. It affects several teeth rather than one, typically in a symmetrical distribution such as both upper canines or the premolars on both sides. It is triggered mainly by cold, sometimes by air. It stops the moment the trigger goes. And it varies from week to week, better some days, worse others.

That variability is a genuinely useful clue. Sensitivity fluctuates. Decay does not improve.

A cavity: what it looks like

Decay is bacteria dissolving tooth structure and moving inwards. Once the enamel surface is breached, there is a physical route to the dentin, so triggers reach the nerve more directly.

The pattern is different. It is usually one tooth, and you can often point to it. Sweet foods provoke it as reliably as cold does, which is not typical of simple sensitivity. Food packs into the area and it aches until you clean it out. You may feel a rough edge or a catch with your tongue or floss, or floss may shred in the same spot every time. And it gets steadily worse over weeks rather than fluctuating.

The floss test

If floss consistently frays, tears, or catches at one particular contact point, that is worth investigating. It very often means a rough cavity margin or a broken filling edge, and it is one of the few things you can reliably detect yourself.

The uncomfortable part: early cavities have no symptoms

This is the piece that undermines the whole idea of waiting until something hurts. Decay begins in the outer enamel, which has no nerve supply at all. There is nothing to feel. It can progress through enamel and into the outer dentin while remaining completely silent.

By the time a cavity is reliably producing symptoms, it is usually well into the dentin and closer to the nerve. That is the difference between a straightforward filling and a conversation about root canal treatment or a crown. Nothing about the patient's behaviour changed, only the timing of when they noticed.

Decay between the teeth is the clearest example. It is completely hidden from view, cannot be probed directly, and is only detectable on an X-ray. Two small check-up radiographs routinely find decay that neither the patient nor I could see any other way.

What happens at the appointment

Working out which of these you have is not guesswork. I look at the surfaces with magnification and good light, probe carefully, take X-rays where indicated, and test the tooth with cold to see how the nerve responds and, importantly, how long the response lasts. I check how your teeth meet, because a high contact mimics decay convincingly. I tap the tooth, since tenderness to tapping suggests inflammation has reached beyond the tooth itself. And if a crack is suspected, I have you bite on a wedge to reproduce it.

Between those, the picture is usually clear within a few minutes, and the treatment for each is completely different. That is why guessing at home has limited value beyond deciding how urgently to come in.

Do not wait on these

Swelling of the face, gum, or under the jaw. Pain that stops you sleeping. Fever alongside toothache. A bad taste with pressure around one tooth. Difficulty opening your mouth or swallowing. A severe toothache that suddenly stopped on its own. Any of these warrant same-day attention.

The practical answer

If it is sharp, brief, affects more than one tooth, and comes and goes, it is most likely sensitivity, and a sensitivity toothpaste plus a mention at your next check-up is a sensible plan. If it is one tooth, if sweet things set it off, if it lingers, or if it is getting worse week on week, book an appointment rather than waiting.

And if you are genuinely unsure, that in itself is a reasonable reason to be seen. An examination takes twenty minutes. A cavity left long enough to become obvious takes considerably more than that to fix.

Questions patients ask me about this

Very early enamel demineralisation, the chalky white stage before a hole forms, can genuinely remineralise with fluoride and better cleaning. Once the surface has broken and bacteria are inside the tooth, no. Enamel has no blood supply and cannot repair a cavity, so it needs cleaning out and filling.

Sweet things draw fluid out of the exposed dentin tubes by osmosis, and that fluid movement is what the nerve registers. A sugar response is a more suspicious sign than a cold response alone, because it usually means there is a route into the dentin rather than just a thin covering.

Sometimes, if it is on a front surface or the chewing surface of a molar and already well developed. Decay between teeth, which is the most common location in adults, is hidden by the neighbouring tooth and is only visible on an X-ray. A dark line on a chewing surface may be a stain rather than decay, which is why we probe and image rather than judge by eye.

Not always immediately. A small area confined to enamel can sometimes be monitored with fluoride and improved cleaning, with an X-ray in six months to check it has not progressed. Once it has passed into dentin, it will keep advancing, and filling it while it is small keeps the treatment simple.

Because the nerve inside the tooth can die. That relieves the pain, but the infection remains and usually resurfaces later as swelling or an abscess. A severe toothache that resolves on its own without treatment is not reassuring, it is a reason to be seen.
