Clear Aligners vs Braces: How to Actually Choose

US Dr. Usha Sribollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics June 30, 2026 5 min read

Both move teeth, and both work. The honest comparison is not about which is better in the abstract, but about what your particular teeth need to do, how much of the work you are willing to take responsibility for, and what you want the finish to look like. Those three things point most people fairly clearly in one direction.

The short version

  • Fixed braces handle complex movement, severe rotation, and large bite corrections more predictably than aligners.
  • Aligners are removable, far easier to clean around, and nearly invisible, but they only work while they are in your mouth.
  • Aligners typically need twenty to twenty-two hours of wear a day. Taking them out for meals and coffee adds up quickly.
  • Costs overlap more than most people expect. The bigger difference is usually case complexity, not the appliance itself.
  • Whichever you choose, a retainer afterwards is not optional. Teeth drift back throughout life if nothing holds them.

What each one is doing mechanically

Teeth are not set in concrete. Each sits in bone, suspended by a ligament, and sustained gentle pressure causes the bone to remodel: it dissolves on the side being pushed towards and rebuilds behind. That is how any orthodontic treatment works, and it is why the process takes months rather than weeks.

Fixed braces bond a bracket to each tooth and connect them with a wire. The wire wants to return to its original shape, and in doing so it applies continuous, three-dimensional control to every tooth. Because the brackets are attached, the force never stops, and the orthodontist can build very specific movements into the wire.

Clear aligners are a sequence of thin plastic trays, each shaped slightly differently from your current position. Every tray applies pressure towards the next step, and you progress through them roughly weekly. Small tooth-coloured attachments are usually bonded to some teeth to give the plastic something to grip, because plastic alone cannot easily rotate or lift a tooth.

Where fixed braces are genuinely better

Aligner technology has improved enormously, and it now handles far more than it did a decade ago. But there are still situations where brackets and a wire are the more predictable choice, and it is worth being told so plainly.

Severely rotated teeth, particularly premolars, are difficult for plastic to grip and turn. Teeth that need substantial vertical movement, either bringing a tooth down or pushing it up into the bone, are much easier with a wire. Large bite corrections, significant crowding requiring extractions, and cases needing the closure of extraction spaces generally go faster and more reliably with fixed appliances. Impacted teeth that need to be brought into the arch essentially require them. And crucially, braces do not depend on the patient remembering anything, which for teenagers is often the deciding factor.

Where aligners are genuinely better

Mild to moderate crowding and spacing, relapse after previous orthodontic treatment, and cases where the main aim is aligning the visible teeth are all well within their range, and the experience is considerably more pleasant.

The practical advantages matter more day to day than people anticipate. You can take them out, so you brush and floss normally, which makes a real difference to gum health during treatment. No food restrictions. No brackets scraping the inside of your cheek. Nothing to break at an awkward moment. Fewer appointments. And they are close to invisible, which for adults in professional settings is frequently the entire reason for choosing them.

There is also a planning advantage. Because the whole sequence is designed digitally in advance, you can usually see a simulation of the predicted end result before you commit.

The wear-time question people underestimate

Twenty-two hours a day leaves two hours for everything you eat and drink, plus the time the trays are out while you clean them. Three meals, a coffee, and a snack consumes that easily. Aligners do not fail because they cannot move teeth. They fail because they were sitting in a case on the kitchen counter.

Talking about cost honestly

I am not going to publish price ranges, because they vary by case complexity, by practice, and by what your insurance covers, and a number in a blog post tends to mislead more than it informs. What I can tell you is how to interpret a quote.

The main driver of cost is how much movement is needed and for how long, not which appliance is used. Simple cases are cheaper in either system. Complex cases are more expensive in either system. When aligners and braces are quoted very differently for the same mouth, it usually means the two plans are not attempting the same thing, and that is the question to ask.

Also ask what is included: are refinement trays covered if the result needs fine tuning, are retainers included or extra, and are replacements covered if you lose one. Those items are where quotes that looked comparable stop being comparable.

Orthodontic benefits are usually a separate category in dental insurance from routine care, often with a lifetime maximum rather than an annual one, and frequently with an age limit. Check that before you plan, because it materially affects the decision.

The part everyone skips: retention

Teeth move throughout your entire life. That is not a failure of treatment, it is normal biology, and it happens to people who have never had braces too. The ligament that suspends each tooth has an elastic memory, and the tissues around a tooth that has just been moved take a long time to reorganise.

So a retainer is part of the treatment, not an accessory to it. Usually that means wearing it full time for a few months, then nights only, then long-term maintenance a few nights a week. A bonded wire behind the front teeth is an option that removes the remembering entirely, and it is worth discussing.

The most common regret I hear from adults considering treatment is not about which appliance they chose the first time. It is that they stopped wearing the retainer at twenty and are now paying to fix the same problem twice.

Before either one: get the foundation checked

Not a good time to start

Active gum disease, untreated decay, or an unrestored broken tooth should all be dealt with first. Moving teeth applies force to the bone that supports them, and applying that force to an inflamed or unstable foundation can accelerate bone loss. Straight teeth on a compromised foundation is a poor trade.

This is also where my own training is relevant. If some of your teeth are heavily restored, crowned, missing, or worn, the orthodontic plan and the restorative plan need to be designed together. Moving teeth into a position that makes a future crown or implant harder to place is an avoidable and fairly common mistake. Deciding the final position first, then the movement to get there, is the right order.

So which should you choose?

If your case is mild to moderate, you are motivated, and appearance during treatment matters to you, aligners are an excellent choice and you will probably enjoy the process more.

If your case involves significant crowding, rotations, extractions, or a substantial bite correction, or if you know honestly that you will not keep trays in for twenty-two hours a day, fixed braces will give you a better result for the same money and time.

The most useful thing you can do is get an assessment that tells you what your teeth actually need to do, rather than starting from which appliance you would prefer. Come in, let us take a proper look, and the choice usually becomes obvious.

Questions patients ask me about this

Undiagnosed gum disease or decay treated with aligner pressure can cause damage that is much harder to fix than the original crookedness. If cost is the issue, discuss it openly with a local dentist rather than removing supervision from the process.

That can look good and function no better. Ask specifically what the plan does about your bite, not just your smile.
