Morning Headaches and a Tight Jaw? You May Be Grinding at Night

US Dr. Usha Sri Bollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics August 5, 2026 5 min read

The headache is there before you are properly awake. Temples, sometimes behind the eyes, sometimes running down into the jaw or the ear. You take something, it fades by mid-morning, and you think nothing more of it until it happens again. If that pattern is familiar, your jaw muscles are a reasonable place to start looking.

The short version

  • Bruxism means clenching or grinding your teeth, and most people who do it at night are completely unaware of it.
  • The clearest clues are morning headaches at the temples, a tight or tired jaw on waking, and flattened or chipped tooth edges.
  • Night-time grinding generates far more force than chewing does, because the protective reflexes that limit your bite are dampened during sleep.
  • A custom night guard protects the teeth but does not stop the grinding. It is a shield, not a cure.
  • Stress, disrupted sleep, some medications, caffeine, alcohol, and an uneven bite are the main contributors worth addressing.

Why the headache comes from your teeth

The main muscle that closes your jaw is the masseter, and it is one of the strongest muscles in the body relative to its size. Alongside it, the temporalis muscle fans out across your temple. When you clench, that temporalis contracts, and hours of sustained contraction overnight leaves it in exactly the state any overworked muscle would be in: tight, tender, and aching.

That is why grinding headaches have such a specific character. They are usually at the temples, they are present on waking rather than developing through the day, and they ease as the muscle relaxes over the morning. People often describe it as a band around the head or pressure behind the eyes.

The forces involved are substantial. During normal chewing, feedback from the teeth and joint limits how hard you bite. During sleep those protective reflexes are reduced, so the muscles can generate considerably more force, sustained for far longer than chewing ever lasts.

How to tell whether you are doing it

Since you are asleep, you have to work backwards from evidence. These are the signs I look for, and several of them you can check yourself.

A simple self-check

Press your fingertips into the fleshy part of your cheeks just in front of your ears, then clench. The muscle that bulges is your masseter. If it is tender to press first thing in the morning but fine by evening, that is a meaningful clue.

What drives it

Bruxism is best thought of as a habit with several contributing inputs rather than a single disease.

Stress and anxiety are the most widely recognised. Periods of pressure at work, poor sleep, and unresolved worry all correlate with more night-time muscle activity, and many patients can date the onset to a specific stressful stretch.

Sleep quality and breathing matter more than most people expect. Grinding often clusters around micro-arousals during sleep, and there is a well-documented association with snoring and sleep apnoea. If grinding comes with loud snoring, waking unrefreshed, or daytime sleepiness, that combination should be assessed properly rather than treated as a purely dental issue.

Substances. Caffeine late in the day, alcohol in the evening, nicotine, and certain prescribed medications including some antidepressants and stimulants all increase it. Alcohol in particular is often misread as helping, because it makes falling asleep easier while making the second half of the night more fragmented.

How your teeth meet. A single tooth sitting slightly high, an old crown that is fractionally too tall, or a bite that has drifted after tooth loss can all give the jaw something to fight against at night. This is the part I can usually assess and adjust directly.

What it costs if it continues

The muscle soreness is the noisy symptom, but it is not the expensive one. Enamel does not regenerate. Once the cusps of the molars are worn flat and the front teeth are shortened, restoring them means crowns or onlays on multiple teeth, which is a large undertaking that is far better prevented.

Grinding also shortens the life of everything already in your mouth. Crowns fracture, veneers debond, fillings crack, and implants take load they were not planned for. If you have invested in restorative work, protecting it is straightforwardly good economics.

Longer term, sustained overload contributes to receding gums, cracked teeth that eventually need root canal treatment or extraction, and jaw joint discomfort.

How it is treated

A custom night guard

This is the mainstay. A hard acrylic guard, made from impressions or a scan of your own teeth, separates the surfaces and spreads the load. Well-made, it is thin, comfortable within a week or two, and lasts years. It also gives useful diagnostic information, because the wear pattern on the guard shows how and where you grind.

It protects the teeth. It does not stop the grinding, and anyone who tells you otherwise is overselling it.

Bite adjustment

If one tooth is taking more contact than it should, easing it by a fraction of a millimetre can settle symptoms that have persisted for months. This is quick and painless when it is the relevant factor.

Addressing sleep and stress

This is where the actual reduction in grinding comes from. Consistent sleep timing, caffeine cut off by early afternoon, less alcohol in the evening, screens down before bed, and genuine treatment for any sleep-disordered breathing. Some patients get real benefit from physical therapy for the jaw muscles, or from simple daytime awareness, since many night grinders also clench through the day at a desk without noticing.

Restoring the damage

Where wear has already changed the shape or height of the teeth, rebuilding them is a planned, staged process. My prosthodontic training is specifically in this area, and the order matters: protect first, stabilise the bite, then restore. Rebuilding worn teeth without controlling the force that wore them down is how expensive dentistry fails early.

Worth prompt attention

A jaw that locks open or closed, pain severe enough to limit eating, sudden inability to bring your teeth together normally, or numbness in the face. These are not routine bruxism and should be looked at without delay.

Where to start

If you wake with headaches and a tight jaw, mention it at your next dental visit even though it does not feel like a dental complaint. The wear on your teeth is objective evidence, and it tells us how long this has been going on and how fast it is progressing. That is a much better starting point than treating the headache alone every morning and hoping it stops.
