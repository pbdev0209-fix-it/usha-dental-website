What to Expect at Your First Visit With Me

US Dr. Usha Sri Bollineni, DDS MDS, Prosthodontics May 22, 2026 5 min read

Most people arrive at a new dentist braced for a lecture about flossing and a list of expensive work. That is not how I run an appointment. Here is exactly what happens, in order, so there are no surprises.

The short version

  • Plan for about an hour. A first visit is longer than a routine check-up because there is more to establish.
  • Bring a photo ID, your insurance card, a list of your medications, and any previous X-rays if you can get them.
  • You will leave knowing what I found, what needs doing, what is optional, and what it will cost. In writing.
  • Nothing is treated at the first visit unless there is a problem causing you pain and you want it dealt with.
  • If you are anxious about dental appointments, tell us when you book. It genuinely changes how we run the visit.

Before you come in

Four things make the appointment run better. A photo ID and your insurance card, so the front desk can check your coverage before we discuss any costs. A list of your medications, including supplements, because a surprising number of them affect saliva, bleeding, and healing. Any recent X-rays from a previous dentist, which saves you taking new ones. And any questions you have, written down, because people reliably forget them once they are in the chair.

If you are anxious, tell whoever books you. It is not a footnote. It changes how much time we allow and how we start.

The conversation first, not the instruments

We talk before I look at anything. I want to know what brought you in, whether anything is bothering you, what your experience of dentists has been, your medical history, and what you actually want from your teeth. That last question matters more than people expect. Someone who wants to be comfortable and keep things stable needs a different plan from someone who wants their front teeth to look different in photographs, and I cannot guess which you are.

This part takes ten minutes or so and it shapes everything that follows.

The examination

Then I look properly, and it covers more than teeth.

I check each tooth surface with magnification and good light, looking for decay, cracks, wear, and failing fillings or crowns. I measure the gums, which involves gently checking the depth of the space between gum and tooth at several points. Bleeding and depth together tell me whether there is inflammation and whether any bone has been lost.

I examine the soft tissues, which is a screening of your tongue, cheeks, palate, floor of the mouth, and the glands in your neck. This is the oral cancer check, it takes under a minute, and it is one of the more genuinely important parts of the appointment.

I look at how your teeth meet, and at your jaw joint and the muscles around it. Wear patterns tell me whether you clench or grind, which is often the first time a patient hears about it.

And I take X-rays where they are needed, usually a couple of small films to see between the teeth. If you have had recent ones elsewhere, we use those instead.

What I am building

By the end of this I have a baseline. That record is what makes future visits meaningful, because comparing today against a year ago tells me whether something is stable or changing, and stable problems are managed very differently from moving ones.

Cleaning

Most first visits include a clean. Hard deposits are removed with an ultrasonic scaler, which is water and vibration rather than scraping, followed by polishing and often a fluoride varnish.

If your gums are inflamed it can be tender in places, and we go gently and can numb an area if needed. If there is significant deposit below the gumline, a proper deep clean may need its own appointment rather than being rushed, and I would rather tell you that than do half a job.

Then we go through the findings together

This is the part that most often differs from what people are used to. We sit up and talk, and I show you what I am describing on the X-rays and photographs rather than reciting it.

Anything urgent, meaning pain, infection, or something actively deteriorating. Anything that needs doing but is not urgent, with a sense of the timescale. Anything worth watching rather than treating, because not every small finding needs intervention today. And anything purely optional, which is cosmetic work that has nothing to do with health.

I am deliberately clear about which category each item falls into. You are entitled to know the difference between what your teeth need and what would simply be nice, and to make your own decision about the second group.

Costs, before anything is booked

You leave with a written plan and written costs. The front desk will have checked your insurance, so you know what is covered, what your remaining annual maximum is, and what you would pay yourself.

If the total is more than you expected, say so. We prioritise. Staging work across several months is completely normal, and treating the important things now while monitoring the rest is a legitimate plan rather than a compromise.

How long it takes

Part of the visitRoughly
History and what you want10 minutes
Examination and X-rays15 to 20 minutes
Cleaning20 to 30 minutes
Going through findings and costs10 minutes

Call it an hour, occasionally a little more. Routine check-ups afterwards are considerably shorter.

If you have been avoiding this

Plenty of people book a first appointment after several years away, and often after a bad experience somewhere else. If that is you, the useful thing to know is that the first visit does not have to include treatment. It can be a conversation and a look, so that you know where you stand and can decide what to do next at your own pace.

You are also allowed to ask me to stop at any point, to ask what something is before I use it, and to ask why I am recommending something. Those are all reasonable requests and none of them are difficult.

Questions patients ask me about this

No, and I mean that. A large proportion of new patients have not been in a long time, usually because of cost, a bad past experience, or life simply getting in the way. None of that is interesting to me. What is in front of us now is what matters. Being told off does not fix teeth and it makes people less likely to come back.

They are not compulsory, but they are how we see decay between teeth, bone levels, and problems at the roots, none of which are visible to the eye. If you have had recent ones elsewhere we can often use those. If you would rather wait, we will discuss what that means for the reliability of the assessment. Modern digital radiographs use very low doses.

If you are in pain, yes, we will address that. For planned work, most people prefer to have the plan and the costs first and then book. If you would rather get something straightforward done while you are there, say so and we will see what is possible in the time available.

Then we prioritise, which is a normal and useful conversation. I will tell you what genuinely needs doing now, what can reasonably wait and be monitored, and what is entirely optional. Staging treatment over months is very common and there is no problem with it.

Quite a lot, and the most useful thing is knowing in advance. We can allow extra time, explain each step before it happens, agree a hand signal that means stop, avoid starting with anything invasive, and simply spend the first appointment building some trust. Please tell us when you book rather than trying to manage it alone on the day.
